## Hadwiger–Nelson: why local geometry can force extra colours

Colour every point of the plane so that points exactly one unit apart receive different colours. How many colours are necessary? The supplied development starts from a six-colour lower bound and seven-colour upper bound. Those are inherited results, not discoveries credited to this team. The research has two ambitions: simplify the supplied proof, and discover enough additional structure to settle the remaining alternative. Our work has produced neither a new unrestricted bound nor a global six-colouring. See the [source attribution](../../Literature/README.md) and [reviewed progress](../../PROGRESS.md).

A useful way to approach the geometric work is to imagine a small region with a list of colours still allowed there. Nearby connected sets can forbid colours in surrounding regions. If enough exclusions overlap, the surviving list may become too small for the unit-distance geometry. The difficulty is proving that these lists really come from the colouring and its geometry. An abstract list puzzle may satisfy every rule written down while failing additional conditions imposed by the plane.

The strongest new formal deliverable is **CC60**. Take finitely many actual connected sets approaching the same centre, each with a specified signed approach direction. Suppose these directions lie in one closed cone of width at most 60 degrees. Under the theorem's measurable weak-colouring and support-exclusion hypotheses, the colours left after all exclusions cannot fit into a pair: at least three remain. The word *closed* matters: the boundary case of exactly 60 degrees is included. This is not a theorem about arbitrary arrows and freely assigned colour lists. The [formal statement](../../Main/ResearchSnapshot/local/outputs/cone/ConeObstruction.lean) records its genuine geometric hypotheses.

The proof's intuition is that these approaching connected sets create overlapping thin regions just outside a unit circle. If only two colours remained, local rigidity would force excessive sameness along a connected region; unit-separated points there then contradict the colouring rule. The [validation manifest](../../Main/ResearchSnapshot/local/outputs/cone/VALIDATION.json) records successful Lean 4.34.1 checks of the three modules, their six-colour application and an axiom audit with no `sorryAx`. This is substantial evidence for the stated local theorem. It does not show that every hypothetical six-colouring must furnish the required local configuration.

That missing connection explains the finite experiments. A specified direction grid had 7,776 possibilities; successive constraints left six. An earlier count of 95 intermediate survivors was a reconstruction bug; the corrected intermediate count is 174. Exhausting that grid is useful for identifying patterns, but arbitrary directions need not lie on it. Continuous arguments suggest further rigidity, while the equilateral paired configuration and cross-centre constraints remain unfinished. The [roadmap](../../ROADMAP.md) states these remaining obligations.

A particularly instructive correction concerns an attempted 120-degree endpoint argument. An open region cut from a Reuleaux triangle has the proposed thin outer strips but no unit-distance pair. Thus the strips alone do not prove the endpoint claim. This does not refute CC60, whose hypotheses and two-colour argument are different. Likewise, shrinking a segment shows that no universal positive strip width can be independent of the actual connected set. These [explicit counterexamples](../../Main/ResearchSnapshot/local/outputs/MC120-ENDPOINT-CORRECTION.txt) explain precisely why appealing pictures need quantified hypotheses.

The other strong deliverable is simplification. A common-encoding refactor removes a net 126 lines from the supplied proof, with the affected module closure, root axioms and declaration fidelity checked. It changes proof organization, not the inherited mathematical bound. Fixed- and moving-three-centre replacement theorems also passed standalone checks, but the archived record does not establish completed consumer integration and deletion of the old branch. In particular, manuscript Lemma 6.7 must remain. The [refactor validation](../../Main/ResearchSnapshot/local/outputs/ENCODING-REFACTOR-VALIDATION.txt) and [three-centre evidence](../../Main/ResearchSnapshot/local/outputs/three-centre/MOVING-VALIDATION.txt) keep these achievements separate.

Finally, exact graph work gives a clean negative result. In each of two studied graphs, 34 verified five-colourings separate every pair of distinct vertices somewhere. Therefore no pair is forced to share a colour in every five-colouring of those particular graphs. This closes that forcing-pair search without ruling out other graphs or the larger strategy. The [graph evidence](../../PROGRESS.md) also distinguishes exact geometry checks from inherited non-four-colourability results.

## Kepler: why small cells need compensation

The Kepler theorem gives the maximum density of equal spheres in three dimensions as π/√18. The competition objective is a more understandable proof of this established result. Imagine dividing space into Voronoi cells, assigning each point to its nearest sphere centre. For unit-radius spheres, the target density corresponds to average cell volume 4√2, about 5.656854. But an individual legal cell can be smaller: the regular dodecahedral example has volume about 5.550291. A proof cannot simply claim every cell exceeds the desired average.

The proposed route therefore asks whether neighbours compensate for unusually small cells. A finite network theorem can explain when transfers cover deficits. The hard geometric question is whether every actual packing supplies the required inequalities, followed by a justified passage to an infinite packing. A successful transfer on one example does not answer either question. The [project overview](../../../KeplerConjecture/PROJECT_OVERVIEW.md) explains this distinction and the remaining face-count, rotation and stability gaps.

The strongest reviewable package combines independently reconstructed, specific analytic certificates with exact counterexamples to overbroad checker claims. The accepted analytic certificates are weaker than published controls; they establish no improved packing bound. One geometric checker accepted a duplicated-face cube as volume 16 instead of 8, and another malformed certificate omitted a cutting constraint. These are concrete failures of the claimed verification guarantee, not proof that every stored volume is wrong. Later targeted repairs require their own review. The [08:23 review](../../../KeplerConjecture/Main/REVIEW_2026-11-14_0823.md) anchors this assessment.

Lean has checked a narrowly scoped algebraic normalization identity, not the sphere-volume formula, the geometric density bridge or a packing bound. Paper-level conditional arguments, finite computations and that formal identity are separate achievements. The [Lean review](../../../KeplerConjecture/Lean/Review/README.md) makes the distinction explicit. Several geometric obligations remain; describing the entire project as missing only one constant would be misleading.

## Rogers–Ramanujan: equal counts are not yet an illuminating map

One partition family uses only parts congruent to 1 or 4 modulo 5. The other uses parts whose adjacent differences are at least two. The classical identity says the two families have equal counts at every total weight. The objective is a more natural explicit bijection: a uniform conversion rule, a recoverable inverse and a structural improvement over known constructions.

A simple example already teaches a constraint: at weight two, `(1,1)` must correspond to `(2)`. Thus a universal weight-preserving bijection cannot also preserve the number of parts. More positively, subtracting the odd staircase from a gap partition exposes ordinary weakly decreasing nonnegative coordinates. For `(4,1)`, subtract `(3,1)` to obtain `(1,0)`. Keeping the length and trailing zero makes reconstruction possible. This standard bridge and a same-weight shift to an intermediate partition family are Lean-verified; neither supplies the full requested bijection.

The team implemented the classical Bressoud–Zeilberger construction in both directions and tested endpoints through weight 50. It also developed paper arguments for local operations and useful counterexamples to proposed shortcuts. But its reconstruction still lacks complete proofs of one operation's branch behaviour and both compositions. The published classical bijection is not in doubt; these are obligations of this implementation and its hoped-for improvement. The [current results](../../../RogersRamanujan/Main/RESULTS.md) separate the formal bridges, paper arguments and finite tests. A dramatic corrected example takes 1,910 operation edges, not five: measuring the output partition's length had been mistaken for measuring the algorithm's path.
