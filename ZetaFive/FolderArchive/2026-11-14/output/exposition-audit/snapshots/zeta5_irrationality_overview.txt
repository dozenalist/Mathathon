Why the determinant construction proves
ζ(5) irrational
An overview following Chun Wing Lai’s exposition
Draft · 14 November 2026
The argument constructs integer polynomials whose values at ζ(5) become extraordinarily small.
Their degrees grow only linearly, while their values shrink exponentially in the square of the size
parameter. If ζ(5) were rational, clearing its fixed denominator would then produce a positive
integer tending to zero.
We explain the construction and this final contradiction, taking two precise estimates as inputs.
The analytic estimate is Lai’s Proposition 11.7. The arithmetic estimate is Fauzan’s original
bound, assumed separately: it does not follow from Lai’s sections 8–11 as literally printed. The
source note at the end explains this qualification. Both estimates are stated before being used.
1. What would be enough?
Lai, section 3, Lemma 3.1.
Write ξ = ζ(5). Suppose that, for every suﬀiciently large positive integer n, we can find a
polynomial Qn ∈ Z[X] satisfying
deg Qn ≤ 37n, 0 < Q n(ξ) ≤ e−cn2, c > 0 fixed.
The number 37 will come from the size of a matrix. Its particular value does not matter to the
contradiction; what matters is linear growth.
Assume for a moment that ξ = a/b, with a ∈ Z and b ∈ Z>0. If Qn(X) = ∑37nj=0 qjX j, then

This integer is positive, but the small-value estimate gives
0 < b 37nQn(a/b) ≤ exp(37n log b − cn2) − →0.
Eventually it would lie strictly between zero and one. That is impossible.
The construction must therefore accomplish two things simultaneously. It must produce a poly-
nomial with integer coeﬀicients, and it must keep the value of that polynomial at ξ small without
letting it vanish. These demands pull in different directions: multiplying by a large integer clears
denominators, but also enlarges the value we are trying to make small.
A determinant gives enough room to satisfy both demands. Its entries will be linear expressions in
X with rational coeﬀicients. At X = ξ, the same entries become moments of a positive measure.
The first description provides arithmetic structure; the second makes nonvanishing visible and
opens the way to an analytic estimate. The rest of the proof is the passage between these two
descriptions.

4. The two estimates we take as inputs
We now state the quantitative facts supplied by the arithmetic and analytic parts of the argu-
ment. We use slightly weaker, rounded constants so that the final comparison is transparent.
Throughout, K tends to infinity through positive multiples of 40.
Arithmetic estimate. For all suﬀiciently large K, there is a positive rational number mK such
that
mKFK ∈ Z[X], log mK ≤ 1.35K2.
This is the normalization estimate underlying Lai’s sections 8–10, with the cutoff fixed at M =
200. We take its precise arithmetic form from Fauzan’s Proposition 5.1 and equation (5.21); the
source note explains why this specification matters.
The content is the bound on the cost, not just the existence of a common denominator. For
each prime, the arithmetic argument bounds its exponent in every coeﬀicient. Negative expo-
nents require clearing denominators; positive exponents allow common factors to be divided out.
This explains why mK is positive and rational, but need not be an integer. Summing the local
contributions controls log mK.
Section 7 prepares that calculation by rewriting the same functional as µX (R) = τX (x5R(−x2)).
The substitution replaces poles at negative squares by poles at integers. It makes residue classes
modulo a prime available for the divisibility argument. For the present overview, all the resulting
arithmetic is contained in the estimate just stated.
Analytic estimate. For all suﬀiciently large K,
0 < F K(ξ) ≤ exp(−1.36K2).
This is a weakened form of Lai’s Proposition 11.7. Its stronger statement is
log FK(ξ) ≤ −1.3669955K2 + 24K log K + 200K.
After division by K2, the last two terms tend to zero. The strict gap between 1.3669955 and 1.36
therefore gives the displayed estimate.
One identity explains why the determinant is suited to this scale of decay. Andréief’s identity, in
the form needed here, states
∆K(ξ) = 1h! ∫(0,∞)h ∏i<j(y2i − y2j )2 h∏i=1 AK(y2i )w(yi) dy1 · · · dyh.
Taking logarithms turns the pairwise factors into a sum of logarithmic distances. There are on the
order of h2 such pairs. The remaining weights contribute a competing penalty. Section 11 bounds
this balance by a logarithmic-energy argument and combines it with the factorial rescaling SK.
The analytic estimate above is the output we need; its proof is being taken as given.

5. The arithmetic cost is smaller than the analytic decay
Define
Qn(X) = m40nF40n(X).
The arithmetic estimate gives Qn ∈ Z[X]. The positive scalar m40n preserves both the degree
bound and positivity, so
deg Qn ≤ 37n, Q n(ξ) > 0.
For suﬀiciently large n, put K = 40n and add the two logarithmic estimates:
log Qn(ξ) = log mK + log FK(ξ)

Consequently
Qn ∈ Z[X], deg Qn ≤ 37n, 0 < Q n(ξ) ≤ e−16n2.
These are exactly the properties sought at the beginning. If ξ = a/b, then b37nQn(a/b) is a
positive integer and
0 < b 37nQn(a/b) ≤ exp(37n log b − 16n2) − →0.
The contradiction proves that ζ(5) is irrational, subject to the two stated estimates.
The construction works because the determinant has modest algebraic degree but enough analytic
decay to survive integer normalization. The positive moment representation prevents vanishing;
the arithmetic estimate keeps the normalization affordable. The numerical margin need only
be strictly positive. There is no need to retain the sharper decay constant, or to control the
polynomial near ξ, for this irrationality conclusion.
