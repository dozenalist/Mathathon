**We have a useful, checked foundation, but the main research breakthrough is still missing. Full completion within 24 hours looks unlikely.**

1. **The goal.** Find a simple, reversible rule connecting two kinds of partitions: sums using numbers such as 1, 4, 6, 9, …, and sums whose successive parts differ by at least two. The total must stay unchanged. Known bijections already exist; the Mathathon asks for a convincingly more natural construction.

2. **What we have proved.** We formalized standard “staircase” transformations that convert one side into an easier intermediate form and back. Lean 4.35 checks the inverse laws and weight identities. We also proved elementary restrictions: a solution cannot always preserve the number of parts or the largest part.

3. **What we have implemented and tested.** We reconstructed the [classical Bressoud–Zeilberger bijection](https://sites.math.rutgers.edu/~zeilberg/mamarimY/rrDG.pdf). Saved tests succeed for every weight through 50, and the latest regression suite passes all 13 checks. This provides a working reference and a way to test ideas. Finite tests do not prove correctness for every input.

4. **What the exploratory work achieved.** We established useful local lemmas, found formulas for selected families, and ruled out some proposed patterns. Independent review also caught incorrect proof labels, a faulty test, and confusion between intermediate and final partitions. Those corrections improved reliability; the family results still need further proof and scope checks.

5. **What remains.** Our complete proof of the reconstructed reference algorithm still has gaps. More importantly, we do not yet have a convincing new construction with a recoverable inverse. Completing the reference proof would certify our reconstruction of known mathematics; it would not itself establish the requested improvement.

**My rough estimate is a 5–15% chance of completing the full goal within 24 hours of active, focused research.** This is a subjective judgment based on the current artifacts, not a statistically calibrated probability. The main reason is the absence of a strong candidate construction; completion requires a conceptual advance as well as verification.

A rigorous partial result is considerably more plausible. I would use the next 4–6 research hours to develop one concrete candidate rule and its inverse, then reassess. A useful infinite-family bijection or a proved simplification would be a credible fallback, subject to checking its novelty.
