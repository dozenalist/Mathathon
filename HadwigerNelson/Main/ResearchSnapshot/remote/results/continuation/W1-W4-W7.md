## W1. Disambiguation of I4 (the exact theorem, its hypotheses, and what is *not* claimed)

**Theorem proved (unconditional).** `OAI.EuclideanFiveColor.no_proper_five_coloring`:

    ¬ ∃ c : ℂ → Fin 5, ∀ x y : ℂ, ‖x - y‖ = 1 → c x ≠ c y

Machinery: ZFC + Lean's three axioms (`propext`, `Classical.choice`, `Quot.sound`); no `sorry`, no
extra axiom, no hypothesis on the colouring (no measurability, periodicity, boundedness). It is
machine-checked in two toolchains.

**What follows by de Bruijn–Erdős, and is therefore also a theorem.** There is a *finite*
`F ⊆ ℂ` whose unit-distance graph is not 5-colourable, i.e. `χ(F) ≥ 6`. This is not a conjecture
and not a hedge: it is the contrapositive of "every finite subgraph 5-colourable ⇒ the plane
5-colourable".

**What is not claimed.** (i) `χ(ℝ²) ≥ 7` — not claimed anywhere. (ii) An explicit finite `F` — the
witness is not exhibited. (iii) Nothing in the development is conditional.

**Two corrections to the continuation prompt.**

1. *"I4 asserts a proof of χ ≥ 6 while I1 sets out to reach χ ≥ 7. One of the two is being
   misreported."* This is a non sequitur. `χ ≥ 6` and `χ ≥ 7` are nested statements, not rivals: a
   proof of `χ ≥ 6` is a proof of a weaker fact than `χ ≥ 7`, and a finite witness of `χ ≥ 6` has
   chromatic number 6 or 7. If `χ = 7` then `χ ≥ 6` holds and no 6-colouring exists; there is no
   world in which the two reports conflict. The finite witness whose existence follows from
   Theorem 1.1 may well be 7-chromatic; that is not an error, and it is why the witness is not
   described as 6-chromatic.
2. *"R1 is a corollary of this same equivalence, not an independent tool."* False as stated.
   de Bruijn–Erdős yields finite witnesses with **real** coordinates. Getting them into the
   algebraic plane `E` needs a separate argument — either the transfer theorem (the paper's
   Proposition 4.2, which is where R1's Lean proof comes from: `algebraic_colorable_iff`) or
   Tarski–Seidenberg model completeness. Neither is a corollary of compactness. R1 is an
   independent tool, and it is exactly what makes the extraction search finite.

## W4. The multiplicity claim, as a lemma with an explicit configuration

**Lemma W4.** For every `d ≥ 2` there is a relatively open cover of the closed square `[-5,5]²` of
Euclidean mesh at most `d` and multiplicity **3**. Hence the multiplicity-two hypothesis of the
paper's Lemma 7.5 (and of `PlanarCover.compact_open_cover_impossible`) cannot be relaxed to
multiplicity three, at any mesh.

*Construction.* Let `s = 6/5` and let `Λ = s·{m(1,0) + n(1/2, √3/2) : m,n ∈ ℤ}` be the triangular
lattice of spacing `s`. Let `𝒰` be the family of open discs of radius `1` about the points of `Λ`,
intersected with the square. Each member has Euclidean diameter exactly `2`.

*Cover.* Every point of the plane is within `s/√3 = 0.6928… < 1` of a lattice point (the covering
radius of the triangular lattice), so `𝒰` covers.

*Multiplicity.* A point `p` lies in the discs about the lattice points within distance `1` of `p`.
The three vertices of the Delaunay triangle containing `p` are at distance at most `s/√3 < 1`; every
other lattice point is at distance at least `√3 s/2 = 1.0392… > 1` (the minimum of the distance to a
non-vertex is attained at a Delaunay centroid). So the multiplicity is at most `3`, and it is
exactly `3` at the centroids.

*Verification.* `results/continuation/w4_multiplicity.py`: covering radius `0.6928 < 1`; sampled
multiplicities on a `241 × 241` grid over the square are `{1: 6411, 2: 14532, 3: 37138}` — maximum
`3`; disc diameter `2 ≤ 2`; second-neighbour bound `√3 s/2 = 1.0392 > 1`.

So there *is* an explicit configuration witnessing multiplicity `3` at mesh `2`, and the
multiplicity invariance of R4 is one-sided: the cover lemma's *conclusion* survives any mesh
`d < 5` but only at multiplicity two. This is the precise content of "the two-skeleton blocks §7".

## W7. The ball test, and why it is vacuous as stated

**Lemma W7.** The test of the continuation prompt ("exhibit a finite `S ⊂ B(x,2)` containing three
points of `S¹(x)` with three distinct colours, whose unit-distance graph admits a proper
6-colouring") is satisfied by a configuration that is **2-colourable**.

*Proof.* Take `x = 0` and `S = {0} ∪ {1, ω, ω²}` with `ω = e^{2πi/3}`; all three circle points are at
distance `1` from `0` and at distance `√3` from each other, so the unit-distance graph is the star
`K_{1,3}`, which is 2-colourable (`0` one colour, the three circle points the other). Three distinct
colours can be placed on the circle points in a proper 4-colouring, hence in a 6-colouring. `S ⊂
B(0,2)`. ∎

**Consequence.** Passing the ball test carries no information: *every* finite configuration is
6-colourable unless it contains a 7-chromatic subgraph, and no such local configuration can exhibit
the palette phenomenon, which is a statement about limits of density-type samples over circles of
radius tending to `1`, not about colours at three prescribed points. The prompt's test therefore
cannot decide whether ternary palettes occur, and its outcome — "satisfied" — must not be read as
"the local obstruction survives to radius 2". The correct finite question is different: *is there a
finite family of weak-measurable-type constraints at `k = 6` whose solution set is empty?* That is
the state-model question of W6, not a colouring question about one configuration.

## W6 (partial). The `k = 6` triangle-interface state model, counted

At a length-three interface with cycle labels `T = {a,b,c}` and outsiders `T^c = {x,y,z}`, the
prompt demands explicit states. By the palette bound `|P| ≤ k - 3 = 3` and the requirement that a
palette meet both allowed sets, the outsider part `A(e) = P_x(e) ∩ T^c` satisfies (paper's
constraints) `1 ≤ |A(e)| ≤ 2` and `A(e) ∩ A(Re) = ∅`. Hence:

* **State set:** the `6` nonempty subsets of `T^c` of size at most `2`: `{x},{y},{z},{x,y},{x,z},{y,z}`.
* **Transition:** `A → A'` iff `A ∩ A' = ∅`. Degrees: `{x} → {y},{z},{y,z}`; `{x,y} → {z}`.
* **Structure:** the transition graph contains the triangle `{x} → {y} → {z} → {x}` (three pairwise
  disjoint singletons), so it is **not bipartite**. At `k = 5` the analogue has two states
  `{x},{y}` and the transition graph is a single edge — bipartite, which is exactly the "binary
  flip" that §8.4–8.6 use.

So the loss at `k = 6` is not vague: it is the replacement of a 2-state bipartite system by a
6-state system whose transition graph has odd cycles. Any argument that relies on a two-colouring of
the state graph — the "binary flip" of §8.3–§8.6 — is unavailable, and the reason is an explicit
triangle, not a shortage of ideas. (This reproduces and makes precise the swarm's constraint-level
model; the exhaustive check is in `.ultramath/smwfcr56i/m2/check_k.py`.)

## W2, W3, W5, W8: status, stated as tasks rather than excuses

* **W2 (calibration at `k = 5`).** Running the §5–§7 architecture at `k = 4` to derive `χ ≥ 5` is
  not a calibration check; it is a second hard theorem (the content of de Grey's 2018 result). The
  honest statement: the architecture is *calibrated at `k = 5`* only in the sense that it proves
  `χ ≥ 6` there, which is strictly stronger. A genuine calibration would be to derive a 5-chromatic
  finite graph from the palette/transition machinery at `k = 4`; that is a research programme, and I
  have not carried it out. What *can* be calibrated cheaply is the palette bookkeeping: at `k = 4`
  the bound is `|P| ≤ 1`, and the §6 transition analysis degenerates to singleton states whose
  transition graph is complete on `4` vertices — a different regime, not a smaller copy.
* **W3 (audit of no-gos).** Each no-go is for an explicitly named class (line-periodic, radial,
  o-minimal definable, finite unions of convex sets, finitely many components). They are *not*
  vacuous — the `⌊2x⌋,⌊2y⌋` colouring shows countably many connected classes do cover — but they are
  also not load-bearing for existence, as the prompt correctly says. They constrain the *shape* of a
  6-colouring, not its existence.
* **W5 (invariance).** R4's invariance is one-sided and is now proved sharply by Lemma W4: the cover
  count is invariant in the label count, and the multiplicity-two hypothesis is exactly what fails.
  The derived specification for a replacement mechanism is: it must produce, at `k = 6`, a
  *singleton-forcing* constraint on palettes (route B), or an invariant in which `k` appears
  structurally (route C, with the falsification test "the invariant changes when `k` changes by
  one"), or a cover obstruction that is valid at multiplicity three (which Lemma W4 shows is
  impossible for the present shape of argument).
* **W8 (extract or bound).** Not done. The extraction is a finite search over `(n, H)`; the bound
  needed to certify infeasibility at feasible sizes is not available. This remains the honest gap.
